is there any benefits to staking a cryp coin vs having the money in a high yield savings, bonds, usdg on robinhood, or another income etf?  

Yes. The main benefit of staking a crypto coin over a HYSA, Treasuries/bonds, USDG lending, or an income ETF is that staking gives you both yield and exposure to the coin's price appreciation.

But that also means you're taking considerably more risk to earn that yield.

For example, imagine you have $10,000:

Investment	Example yield	What happens to your $10k?	Main risk
HYSA	~4%	Stays ~$10k + interest	Very low
Treasury	~4–5%	Predictable if held to maturity	Very low
USDG lending	~7% currently	~$1 stablecoin ≈ $1 target	Smart contract/stablecoin/liquidity risk
Income ETF	~5–12%+	Value fluctuates	Stock/strategy risk
SOL staking	~5–7%*	SOL price can move dramatically	Crypto volatility
Higher-yield crypto staking	10–20%+	Coin may crash despite yield	Very high

*Staking rates vary constantly.

Where staking gets interesting

Suppose you put $10,000 into SOL and receive 6% staking rewards.

If SOL's price doesn't move:

$10,000 → ~$10,600

That's not particularly compelling compared with something like Robinhood's current USDG onchain lending, which advertises an estimated 7% APY.

But suppose SOL appreciates 40% during that year.

Roughly:

$10,000 × 1.40 × 1.06 ≈ $14,840

Now staking looks fantastic.

The reverse is equally important. If SOL falls 40%:

$10,000 × 0.60 × 1.06 ≈ $6,360

You earned 6% staking yield while losing roughly 36% of your money.

So I wouldn't really think of staking as an alternative to a savings account. It's closer to:

"I already want to own this crypto long-term, so I might as well earn additional coins while holding it."

That's the strongest argument for staking.

Comparing the choices you mentioned

HYSA: Best for emergency funds or money you absolutely don't want to lose. Current top HYSAs are around 4–4.5%, with FDIC/NCUA protection when eligibility requirements are met.

Treasuries/bonds: Probably the most attractive comparison if your goal is simply safe yield. You can lock in a yield rather than having your HYSA rate change. Treasury yields have actually been rising recently.

USDG on Robinhood: This one is particularly interesting right now. Robinhood is advertising an estimated 7% APY, but there's an important distinction: this isn't a 7% bank account. You're lending USDG onchain through Morpho from a self-custody wallet. It's not FDIC or SIPC protected, withdrawals depend on vault liquidity, and you have smart-contract/onchain risk. Robinhood says insurance may cover certain cyber or smart-contract exploits, but that isn't equivalent to FDIC insurance.

Robinhood Earn / USDG lending details

Income ETF: This occupies a different category. Depending on what you're talking about—JEPI/JEPQ, covered-call ETFs, dividend ETFs, CLO ETFs, etc.—you can get income plus some capital appreciation, but distributions aren't free money. Some strategies sacrifice upside to generate the income.

Crypto staking: Highest potential upside of these because you're getting the staking reward plus the underlying crypto appreciation. But you're also taking substantially more downside risk.

There's another staking advantage that's easy to overlook: you're accumulating more units rather than dollars. If you believe SOL, ETH, or another PoS coin will be worth substantially more five years from now, getting paid 4–7% more coins every year can compound nicely.

For example, 100 SOL staked at an average 6% for five years becomes approximately:

134 SOL

If SOL were eventually worth $500, that's ~$67,000 instead of $50,000 from simply holding 100 SOL.

But that thesis depends overwhelmingly on SOL eventually being worth $500. The staking yield itself isn't doing most of the work.

One more downside: taxes

For a U.S. investor, staking rewards are generally taxable income when you gain control of them, based on their dollar value at that time. The IRS reaffirmed its treatment of staking rewards, and a 2026 Tax Court decision also held staking rewards to be gross income.

Then if the rewarded crypto subsequently appreciates and you sell it, you can also have a capital gain.

So staking can create more tax/accounting complexity than simply holding an appreciating asset.

The question I'd use isn't:

It's:

"Would I buy SOL even if staking paid 0%?"

If the answer is yes, staking it usually makes a lot of sense rather than letting the SOL sit idle.

If the answer is no, buying SOL solely because staking pays 6% doesn't make much sense when you can currently get roughly 4% from insured savings, attractive Treasury yields, or ~7% from USDG lending without taking SOL's price risk. The extra 2% staking yield is tiny compared with the possibility of SOL moving ±30–50%.

I think that distinction matters a lot. There are really three different ideas hiding under "rent hardware for mining," and only one is particularly attractive for what you're trying to build.

NiceHash is primarily a hashpower marketplace, not traditional cloud mining: miners sell computational power and buyers pay to direct that hashpower toward a pool. NiceHash explicitly describes itself as connecting buyers and sellers rather than owning/renting its own mining equipment.

MiningRigRentals is more literal—you rent someone else's rig/hashrate for a specified period and point it at your mining pool. It also lets owners list their hardware for rent; MRR currently charges rig owners 3% of rental revenue.

Then there are "give us $1,000 and we'll mine for you for three years" cloud-mining contracts. That's the category I'd be extremely cautious about. The CFTC/SEC have specifically warned about fraudulent crypto sites claiming to operate mining farms and promising high or guaranteed returns.

The fundamental problem with renting mining power

Even with a legitimate marketplace, there's an economic question I have trouble getting around:

Why would someone rent me $10 worth of expected mining production for $8?

Usually they won't.

The owner of the hardware understands their electricity cost and expected mining revenue. So the rental price generally needs to compensate them for:

electricity + hardware + depreciation + profit

and then the marketplace gets its cut.

For you to make money as the renter, something unusual generally has to happen:

Mining revenue > rental price + pool fees + marketplace costs

That can happen—coin price changes, difficulty changes, temporarily mispriced hashpower, new coin launches, solo-mining luck, etc.—but then we've moved away from the predictable "hydroponics" concept and into another speculative strategy.

That's actually why renting out hardware interests me more than renting hardware in your situation.

Rented at sufficiently high price → sell hashpower

↓
receive crypto

↓

↓
stake

MRR actually allows exactly this sort of configuration: the rig mines to your normal pool when it isn't rented and switches to the renter's configured pool when rented.

Whether anyone would pay enough for a single 3060 Ti to make that worthwhile is another question. 😄 But we could measure it.
